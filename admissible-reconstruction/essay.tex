\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This essay applies the four theorems proven in the monograph
\emph{Admissible Reconstruction} to a more general projection problem:
determining which structural relations survive transformations among
different physical, symbolic, and perceptual realizations. Speech, text,
gesture, typography, music, spatialized audio, and other modalities differ
in the structures they preserve and discard. The central problem is
therefore not merely how a known latent intentional state is projected
into different channels, but how families of physically distinct
presentations can reveal structures that remain invariant across
transformation.

This distinction changes the ontology of representation. An utterance,
its transcription, a particular speaker's performance, a synthesized
voice, and a typographic rendering need not be treated either as wholly
different objects or as interchangeable copies of one object. They may
instead be equivalent with respect to some structures while remaining
distinct with respect to others. Representational identity is therefore
indexed by the relations required to survive. Changing a font may preserve
one family of relations; changing a speaker may preserve another;
translation, paraphrase, summarization, gesture, and cross-modal
substitution preserve progressively different structural subsets.
Cross-modal equivalence consequently requires neither physical resemblance
nor identity of signal. It requires preservation of the relevant
relations.

This formulation also distinguishes invariant identity from presentation
identity and historical event identity. One linguistic work may admit
many performances; one performance may admit several encodings; and one
recorded event may be transcribed, embedded, summarized, classified, or
replayed repeatedly without thereby becoming several historical events.
Conversely, many independent events may instantiate the same discovered
invariant. A representation system that collapses these distinctions
cannot reliably distinguish recurrence in the world from recurrence
introduced by its own processing.

The four theorems constrain this problem jointly. Representation
Invariance determines when physically distinct realizations preserve the
same admissibility structure. Admissibility Preservation determines when
transformations preserve coherent reachable trajectories. Monotonic
Relaxation determines when repair decreases structural inadmissibility
without requiring reconstruction of an inaccessible original.
Non-Identifiability establishes the complementary limit: agreement under
an observation map does not imply identity of source, history, or
presentation whenever that map is non-injective. Its diagnostic corollary
therefore distinguishes correctable observational error from structural
ambiguity.

The resulting framework suggests a theory of \emph{invariance discovery}.
Rather than requiring every invariant to be specified in advance, a system
may compare large families of presentations and identify relations that
persist across changes of speaker, typography, encoding, language,
performance, sensory channel, and environment. Such discovery does not
license destructive abstraction: the presentations and their provenance
remain historically distinct even when they instantiate a common
structure. The system therefore retains both transformation and
difference.

This has consequences for memory and interface design. Persistent
information need not be repeatedly injected into an active symbolic
context in order to remain accessible. A representation may instead
occupy a stable relational position and admit multiple perceptual
realizations. In a spatial auditory environment, for example, files or
tasks may announce location, distance, class, salience, or trajectory
through birdsong, environmental sound, speech, or approaching mechanical
signals. An approaching train can represent an approaching deadline not
because trains resemble tasks, but because both instantiate the same
relevant relational structure: decreasing distance to a consequential
boundary. In this sense, changing modality can become analogous to
changing a font---a change in presentation under which specified
structural relations remain invariant.

The broader claim is that representation, coherence, repair,
reconstruction, memory, and interface are not independent problems.
They are different views of the same question: what remains invariant
under transformation, what historical distinctions must nevertheless be
preserved, and what cannot be reconstructed once a projection has erased
the distinctions required to tell possible histories apart.
